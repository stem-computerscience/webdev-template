# Lesson 1: Exploring Web Pages

**Chapter:** 1 — Creating Webpages
**Duration:** 45 minutes
**Source:** [Code.org Lesson Plan](https://studio.code.org/courses/ai-discoveries-2026/units/3/lessons/1)

## Question of the Day
How can we choose websites based on the needs of a user?

## Overview
To kick off a unit devoted to group problem solving and developing websites for other users, students begin by investigating the design of different websites. Students look at a variety of websites and attempt to match each design with a potential user. Then students choose a user and attempt to prototype a website design for them on paper or in a digital template. To conclude the activity, students consider what it means to be a web designer and create resources for other users.

## Standards
CSTA K-12 Computer Science Standards (2017) — IC: Impacts of Computing

## Objectives
Students will be able to:
- Create a prototype of a web design to meet the needs of a user using the problem-solving process
- Identify features of a web design that match the needs of users
- Understand the steps of the problem-solving process

### Warm Up - Option 1 (for classes not yet introduced to the Problem Solving Process)

**Problem Solving Word Search (10 minutes)**
- *Remarks:* In this unit, we'll be learning how to design websites to solve problems. The word "problem" can refer to lots of different situations.
- *Prompt:* What's an example of a problem you've had to solve recently?
- Circulate & distribute a copy of the Word Search activity guide to each student, face down.
- *Remarks:* When we build websites, we'll also face problems to solve — today we'll learn a strategy for solving problems, starting with a word search.
- *Prompt:* What is the problem you are trying to solve when you do a word search?
- *Discussion Goal:* The problem of a word search is finding all the hidden words in the grid.
- *Prompt:* What strategies could someone use for finding words, and what are the pros/cons of each?
- *Discussion Goal (possible strategies):* scan row by row then column by column; check surrounding letters once a key letter is found; look for less-common letters (Q, X, J, Z); look for letter pairs (double letters, QU); look for more than one word at a time; turn the puzzle upside down.
- Students pick ONE strategy, mark it "#1" in their journals, and try it — they may switch strategies (numbering each new one) if it isn't working.
- Circulate while students search; regroup once most have found several words.
- *Reflection prompts:* Were you successful? How do you know? What strategy did you pick and how did it work? Did anyone change strategies, and why?
- *Teaching Tip:* Consider a quick hand-raise poll on success, or have students pick two reflection questions to answer.

**The Problem Solving Process (5 minutes)**
- Display "The Problem Solving Process" video. Question to consider: What are the 4 steps to the Problem-Solving Process?
- *Teaching Tip:* Turn on closed captioning; use active engagement strategies from the Guide to Curriculum Videos.
- Discuss how the warm-up activity mapped to each step, then reveal the example answers in the slides:
  - **Define:** putting into your own words what the problem of a word search is and what the goal is
  - **Prepare:** brainstorming different strategies that could be used
  - **Try:** picking a strategy and trying to find the words
  - **Reflect:** reflecting on how it went
- *Teaching Tip:* Remind students the process is a cycle — switching strategies mid-task means looping back through Reflect → Prepare → Try.
- *Remarks:* Now let's explore how to use this process when designing websites.

### Warm Up - Option 2 (5 minutes, for classes already introduced to the Problem Solving Process)

**Revisit The Problem Solving Process (5 minutes)**
- *Remarks:* Today we'll remind ourselves about the Problem Solving Process and how it applies to designing websites.
- *Prompt:* What do you remember about the Problem-Solving Process?
- *Discussion Goal:* Lead students through the four steps and what's involved in each.
- *Prompt:* What might each step look like when creating a website?
- *Discussion Goal (possible ideas):*
  - **Define:** deciding what the website is about or who it's for
  - **Prepare:** looking at similar websites, deciding on colors/images, sketching
  - **Try:** creating a basic version without all features
  - **Reflect:** showing it to friends for feedback

### Activity (35 minutes)

**Recommending Websites**
- *Remarks:* This unit we'll learn to create websites, starting with thinking about who we're designing for.
- Group students in pairs; distribute the Web Design for Users activity guide (A/B versions — either is fine).
- *Teaching Tip:* Answer keys/exemplars are available for Verified Teachers.
- Have students read the first page directions: matching users to a website template that suits their needs, using the **Sample Web Pages** level (Level 1) on Code Studio.
- Circulate as pairs work through the matching activity, discussing why each template fits.
- *Teaching Tip:* Value reasoning over "correctness" — each user has an intended template, but students should be able to justify their own choices. Sites are intentionally simple to set expectations for the unit.
- *Share Out:* Discuss which users were easiest/hardest to match; highlight cases where students disagreed and have them explain their reasoning.
- *Assessment Opportunity:* Check that students are reasoning from the described user's needs, not their own preferences.

**Designing a Website**
- *Remarks:* Now design your own website for a client, meeting the needs of that user.
- *Teaching Tip:* This activity may run past the class period — that's okay, it can be revisited later or stretched across a second period.
- Have students flip to the back of the activity guide, choose a user, and sketch a basic website design following the Problem Solving Process (pen and paper or the provided Google Slides template). These are meant to be quick sketches, not polished designs.
- Distribute the "Problem Solving and Design" resource as a reference throughout the unit.
- Circulate to ensure designs match the chosen user's needs, and monitor collaboration/voice-sharing in pairs (useful groundwork for later team projects).
- *(Optional) Share Out:* Trade designs with another pair who picked the same user to compare approaches.
- *Assessment Opportunity:* Use the activity guide to gauge how well students followed each step of the Problem Solving Process — this recurs throughout the unit, especially on projects.

### Wrap Up (5 minutes)
- *Prompt (Reflect):* What's one thing your design does well to meet the user's needs? What's an area that could still be improved?
- Students record answers in the Reflect section of the activity guide.
- *Share Out:* Have students share responses, highlighting similarities even across different users.
- Revisit the Question of the Day: How can we choose websites based on the needs of a user?

**Reflection:** Students answer 5 quick survey questions using the **Check-In Survey** level (Level 2) on Code Studio. Once 5+ students respond, anonymized results are viewable in the Teacher Dashboard. Some questions are repeated at the end of the first project to track growth over the unit.
